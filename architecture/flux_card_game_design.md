# FLUX — the folk card game of the eleven factions

**Status: Proposal** — design doc for the fourth game; cite as intent,
not law. Distilled 2026-07-30 from the FLUX Notion workspace (Design
Foundation v1.1, Character Guide v1.0) plus the design conversation
that moved it into this universe. FLUX is the working title; the
in-world name is an open question (see §10).

The universe at **hand scale**: the tabletop plays the world at
conversation scale, the Circuit at squad scale, Close Contact at limb
scale — this is the scale where you hold your people in your hand and
choose what to put on the table.

---

## 1. What this is

A digital collectible card game set inside the SENTINEL world as a
**folk game the world itself plays** — dealt in Syndicate dens, Covenant
halls, and under-fence markets across the eleven regions. Snap-derived
chassis (locations, energy curve, simultaneous commit-and-reveal),
**collection and ante instead of a roguelike**, and a career that is a
card-shark's tour of the eleven regions.

Two mechanics are the settled heart, carried from the original FLUX
design:

1. **The Snap chassis** — locations that bend the rules, an energy
   curve, hidden simultaneous commitment resolving in public reveals.
2. **Degradation** — losses that permanently cost you cards. Reframed
   for this universe: see §4. You lose cards because you *staked* them.

## 2. What moved in from FLUX, and what deliberately did not

**Kept:** the chassis; the energy-economy archetype and its deck (§7 —
the designer's own Infinite-rank Snap deck, translated); the
location-manipulation and movement archetypes (whose FLUX factions were
already SENTINEL factions wearing masks — the "Architects" shared the
name outright, and the Drifters' founder was THE ETERNAL WANDERER);
"named cards are people, not just mechanics."

**Dropped, with reasons:**

- **The roguelike run structure.** Roguelikes reset; this universe's
  thesis is that nothing resets and nothing is forgotten. The
  degradation the runs carried survives in a stronger, chosen form
  (§4).
- **Phoenix Corps.** Resurrection tech that exempts cards from
  degradation fights both the universe (death is real here) and the
  mechanic this game is built on. Cut whole.
- **Project Zero lore.** FLUX's "ZERO" faction and Team-ZERO ethics
  belong to a different universe. The energy-economy playstyle stays;
  its owner changes (§6).

## 3. The chassis

- **Three locations** per match, each with a rule that bends play.
  Locations are *places in the world* — venues and regions, not
  abstract zones (§8).
- **Six rounds.** Energy rises each round. Cards cost energy, carry
  Power, and speak two tenses: **On Reveal** (once, when it happens)
  and **Ongoing** (as long as it stands).
- **Commit, then reveal.** Both players place cards face-down;
  everything turns over and resolves in public. The genre's core
  keyword is already this universe's core act: the reveal is a witness
  beat. What you held back stays yours; what you committed is on the
  record.
- **Win two of three locations** by total Power.

### The raise and the fold

Snap's cube betting returns diegetically — you raise with *cards*:

- Matches open with an agreed **ante** (stakes tier per table, §4).
- Mid-match, either player may **raise** — naming a specific card from
  their binder to sweeten the pot. "My ORACLE says you're bluffing."
- **Folding is the yield.** Walk away from the table in front of
  everyone, keep what's yours, forfeit the pot. The fold must feel like
  the Circuit's yield: a legible, watched decision to stop — never a
  menu action. Knowing when to walk is a skill the game respects.

## 4. Degradation, transposed: playing for keeps

The original FLUX rule — lose a match, remove a card — was a system
imposing loss. The folk frame restores the older, better version:
**keepsies**. You lose cards because you put them on the table.
Choosing consequences you can live with, as a resource system.

- **Stakes tiers are consent architecture** (design philosophy #2
  wearing a dealer's visor): friendly tables (no stakes — practice,
  story, tutorials), ante tables (pot of cards, agreed up front),
  for-keeps dens (raises legal, sharks present, the good rewards).
  Stakes are visible before you sit. Exposure is always a decision,
  never a lottery.
- **No system ever takes a card you didn't stake.**

### Circulation — degradation with memory

A lost card does not vanish. **Somebody has it.** The dealer you lost
your finisher to carries it in her binder; it turns up in a rival's
deck two regions later; it can be tracked, won back, bought back, or
let go — and the fiction knows the difference. Losses become
geography, and sometimes quests. The world's card pool is closed
enough to feel like matter: cards move, they don't respawn.

This is the universe's oldest promise — *the fighter you spared meets
you again* — expressed in cardboard, and it is what separates this
collector game from every other one: the collection isn't a checklist,
it's a ledger of where everything went.

## 5. Collection — the folk-object fantasy

Cards are physical objects in the fiction, and the systems treat them
that way:

- **Print runs and provenance.** Faction print runs, Witness-pressed
  commemoratives, hand-copied bootlegs, counterfeits, marked decks in
  crooked dens. A card's history — who held it, what tables it crossed
  — is part of the card, inspectable like everything else in this
  universe. Provenance is value.
- **Condition.** Wear is cosmetic and narrative (the crease from the
  night it got won at gunpoint), never statistical — a worn card plays
  at full strength. Honest surfaces: the game state is the text, not
  the damage.
- **Errata mints, never overwrites.** A card, once printed, keeps its
  printed text forever. Balance changes ship as *new print runs* —
  new collectible cards with their own provenance — and the old
  printing stays legal and stays yours. No patch ever reaches into a
  binder. Where a printing genuinely warps play, the world handles it
  the way folk games always have: tables and regions bar it by house
  rule — visible, local, chosen — never by silent global rewrite.
  This is the witness discipline at card scale (history is never
  reinterpreted; the rules stamp refuses exactly this), and it has a
  paper trail: the original FLUX pillars demanded "no meta shifts
  deleting your deck," written by a designer who once quit a card
  game for a year over a retroactive nerf. BREAKER (§7) is printed
  accordingly.
- **Named cards depict real people of the world.** Faction legends,
  Circuit fighters, campaign figures. The folk game is how the world
  gossips about its own history — a card of a fighter you played as in
  the tactical layer is the cross-game canon working at trading-card
  scale.
- **Acquisition is worldly:** won at tables, bought in markets, traded
  with collectors, earned from jobs. No loot boxes; the den IS the
  loot box, and it has a face and a memory.

## 6. Factions as card pools

Every faction plays the way it believes. The three FLUX archetypes map
onto their SENTINEL owners; the rest of the eleven wait their turn.

| FLUX archetype | SENTINEL owner | Identity |
|---|---|---|
| ZERO — "adaptive efficiency, waste nothing" | **Lattice** — *We keep the lights on* | The energy economy: unspent power becomes strength, overloads get cut. The deck is literally about keeping the grid disciplined. |
| The Drifters — "motion is strength" | **Wanderers** — *The road remembers* | Movement, displacement, punishing the static. The FLUX founder card was already named THE ETERNAL WANDERER. |
| The Architects — "control the arena" | **Architects** — *We built this world* | Location manipulation, rule rewriting. The name didn't even need changing. |

Future pools, sketched by philosophy (each is a design brief, not a
promise): **Witnesses** (information — peek at unrevealed cards, replay
effects, punish lies), **Ghost Networks** (absence — face-down play,
profit from the unrevealed), **Steel Syndicate** (leverage — steal
Power, call in debts, everything has a price), **Nexus** (prediction),
**Covenant** (protection and oaths), **Convergence** (transformation),
**Cultivators** (growth over rounds), **Ember Colonies** (survival at
low resources).

## 8. Locations are places

The FLUX location designs carry over as mechanics; their names become
geography — venues in regions, so that *where you're sitting* is part
of the game. Examples of the re-grounding (full pass is future work):

- THE DEADZONE (*all abilities disabled — pure stats*) — keeps its
  name; it reads native.
- FLUX CORRIDOR (*unspent energy feeds cards here*) → a Lattice relay
  floor in the Rust Corridor.
- THE CRUCIBLE (*played cards destroy the weakest here*) → a Syndicate
  pit in the Texas Spine.
- MIRROR HALL (*"enemy" effects hit both sides*) → a Covenant hall in
  the Northern Reaches — their tables enforce symmetry on principle.

**Regional house rules** are a second, separate layer: each of the
eleven regions plays the game slightly differently (what can be
staked, how raises work, which locations show up). Folk games mutate
by geography; eleven regions means eleven dialects of FLUX, learned
the way the Circuit's venues are learned.

## 9. Career, opponents, witness

- **The career is a circuit of tables**: den to den, region to region,
  rivals with readable playstyles and binders full of what they've won
  — the same local-scene structure the Circuit and Close Contact use,
  because that is how this universe organizes ambition.
- **Rivals are card personalities** (the house pattern): the shark who
  never raises first, the collector who won't stake named cards, the
  counterfeiter whose deck is full of lies.
- **The witness layer fits this game best of all** (future work, noted
  now so the design doesn't foreclose it): turn-based, discrete state,
  tiny records. Commit-reveal is the cryptographic shape of witnessed
  hidden information — hash the committed hand, reveal, verify.
  A match record with hidden-then-revealed hands is certifiable at the
  edge with the machinery the Circuit already runs. Card provenance
  (§5) is the same pattern at object scale.

## 10. Open questions

1. **The in-world name.** FLUX is the working title. Leading
   candidate: **COLORS** — the insignia system already runs on
   "declaring colors," THE CARD already prints "COLORS: none
   declared," and a folk game about committing hidden allegiances then
   revealing them wants that name badly. Shawn's call.
2. **How mean is keepsies?** Stakes tiers are the consent answer, but
   the floor needs design: what protects a new collector's first good
   card; whether staked cards can be insured, marked unstakeable, or
   held by faction custom (a Covenant table will not take your last
   named card).
3. **Named cards vs canon.** Cards depict real people of the world —
   how directly they tie to wiki figures and campaign NPCs (a CIPHER
   card exists in FLUX's drafts already) is a lore-integration pass.
4. **Platform.** Turn-based, small state, web-native — the natural
   first consumer of the TS platform direction after the Circuit. Not
   scheduled; named so nobody accidentally builds it in the wrong
   stack.

## 11. Explicitly out of scope for the first design pass

- All eleven faction pools (three archetypes are designed; the rest
  are briefs)
- Multiplayer and trading between real players (the fiction is
  single-player-with-a-living-world first; the witness layer keeps
  the door open)
- Monetization of any kind — this is a game about a world where
  everything has a price, which is exactly why the product must not
- Physical printing (the folk-object fantasy is diegetic; whether it
  ever becomes real cardboard is a different decision with different
  stakes)
